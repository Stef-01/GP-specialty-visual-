The eye clinic is full of eyes that are fine

Diabetic retinopathy is silent until it is late. 01 — The problem

A silent disease, screened by lottery

Diabetic retinopathy has the worst possible combination of properties for a referral system: it is common, it is treatable, and it produces no symptoms until the damage is done. So the people who most need an ophthalmologist have no reason to seek one, and the people who least need one are just as likely to be sitting in the waiting room. The result is a queue sorted by who happened to get a referral, not by whose sight is at risk.

“The referral is not sorted by risk. It is sorted by who managed to keep an appointment forty minutes away for a problem they cannot feel.”

The item is not the barrier. Unlike the eConsult in Explainer 01, this mechanism needs no new MBS item. Items 12326 (all patients, once per 24 months) and 12325 (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients, once per 12 months) already pay a schedule fee of $59.75 for visual acuity plus bilateral non-mydriatic retinal photography, analysed and reported, performed by the practitioner managing the patient's glycaemia. What is missing is the camera in the room, a grading result the GP can act on in the same consultation, and a recall that fires without anybody remembering.

03 — The mechanism

Move the screen to where the patient already is

Nothing here requires a new consultation. The patient is in the room for a diabetes review they already booked; the camera is in the room they are already sitting in; the item that pays for it already exists. What changes is that the sorting happens before the referral instead of after it.

STEP 1

Capture

Non-mydriatic camera in the treatment room. Two images per eye, no drops, about sixty seconds, delegated to a practice nurse under the GP's direction — exactly as the MBS item already contemplates.

Ungradable images are a result, not a failure: they route to mydriasis or to an optometrist, and they are counted.

STEP 2

Grade

An autonomous model returns an international grade and a confidence, in the consultation, not in a letter three weeks later. Regulator-cleared systems report roughly 87–96% sensitivity and 81–91% specificity for more-than-mild retinopathy in pivotal and real-world studies.

Sensitivity is deliberately favoured over specificity: the cost of a false positive is one appointment, the cost of a false negative is a retina.

STEP 3

Dispose

The output the GP acts on is not a diagnosis — it is a disposition: a destination, an urgency and a date, written into the recall system before the patient stands up.

Any symptom — blurring, floaters, sudden change — overrides the grade and is referred regardless.

“The model does not decide what is wrong with the eye. It decides which eyes an ophthalmologist should be looking at this month.”

05 — The second yield

The only microvessels in the body you can photograph directly

This section is the speculative one, and it is labelled as such. Once a retinal photograph exists for 90% of a practice's diabetes population, a second question becomes free to ask: what else is in that image? Oculomics — the study of ocular biomarkers of systemic disease — is an active research field, not a clinical service, and nothing in this section should be treated as standard of care.

What is measurable

Vessel calibre, tortuosity, branching angle and fractal dimension are quantifiable from an ordinary fundus photograph, and they change with systemic vascular disease before symptoms appear.

What is being validated

Deep-learning models predicting cardiovascular risk, chronic kidney disease and other systemic conditions from retinal images have reached prospective and pivotal study stage; a regulated retinal cardiovascular risk score has been through a pivotal trial.

Why the marginal cost is zero

The capture already happened for the retinopathy screen. A second read adds compute, not clinic time, not a device, and not another visit for the patient.

What it would change in the room

The GP is already doing a chronic-disease review. Today the vascular picture comes from blood pressure, lipids and an absolute-risk calculator built from population averages. A retinal vascular read would add a measurement of this patient's small vessels to that conversation — at the same visit, from an image taken for another purpose.

  • It is not a diagnosis, and it does not start or stop a medication on its own.
  • It is not billed, because no item covers it and none should until the evidence supports one.
  • It is a research output, consented separately, reported alongside the retinopathy grade and audited against outcomes.
  • It is the reason to build the imaging base now: the screen justifies itself on sight alone, and the second yield arrives on infrastructure that already paid for itself.

06 — Align the money

Who pays, who earns, and the part that does not add up yet

The honest position: the MBS item covers the read, not the camera. Practice economics on the item alone are thin, and pretending otherwise would be the fastest way to lose a room full of practice managers. The return is at system level, and the capital needs a program.

The ledger, stated plainly

  • Practice, per two-year cycle: 450 screens × $59.75 = $26,887, against ~$14,937 at today's coverage — about +$6k a year in new billing.
  • Practice, against capital: a non-mydriatic camera is a five-figure purchase and the model licence is per read. On the item alone the payback is slow, which is why this is proposed as a program-funded device, not a practice investment.
  • Ophthalmology, per 1,000 diabetes referrals: 610 appointments at $103.95 = $63,410 of specialist time currently spent confirming that a retina is normal.
  • The system: those 610 slots absorb the referable and sight-threatening referrals that are currently queued behind them — the wait falls without a single new ophthalmologist.
  • Medicare: pays more in screening items and less in avoidable specialist attendances, and buys detection in the 40% of the population who are currently not imaged at all. As in Explainer 01, this is a throughput trade, argued as one.

08 — Ask small

One primary health network, twelve months

Enough practices to see a coverage effect and enough images to audit the model against a masked human grade. The trial is designed to measure the two things that would kill the idea: a false-negative rate that lets sight-threatening disease through, and a device that performs unequally across the population it screens.

The ask

$520k

  • 12 general practices in one PHN, ~6,000 people with diabetes
  • $288k — 12 non-mydriatic cameras at ~$24k, program-funded, retained by the practices
  • $43k — autonomous grading licence, per read, plus a 20% masked audit sample
  • $120k — practice nurse training, recall configuration, and a named ophthalmologist of record per practice
  • $69k — independent evaluation, including performance by subgroup

The capital is the ask. The item pays for the read; it does not pay for the camera. That is the honest gap this trial exists to close.

Eight KPIs

  • Screening coverage ≥85% of the practice diabetes population in 12 months
  • Ungradable image rate ≤10%, all with a completed alternative pathway
  • Model sensitivity for referable disease ≥90% against masked ophthalmologist grading
  • Specificity ≥80% on the same audit sample
  • Median image-to-ophthalmology time for sight-threatening grades ≤42 days
  • Share of new eye-clinic diabetes referrals that are grade 2 or worse ≥70%
  • The false-negative check: no sight-threatening disease at the 12-month re-screen in anyone the model graded “no retinopathy” at baseline
  • Coverage among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients at or above the overall rate

09 — What could go wrong

Six risks, six safeguards

The model misses disease

Safeguard: any visual symptom is referred regardless of grade; sensitivity is favoured over specificity at the threshold; a 20% sample is graded blind by an ophthalmologist every month; everyone is re-imaged on schedule, so a miss has a second chance rather than a lifetime.

It performs worse for some patients than others

Safeguard: device chosen on published subgroup performance; accuracy reported by ethnicity, age and image quality quarterly; the equity KPI is coverage and accuracy, not coverage alone; local audit before any scale-up.

Ungradable images become silent failures

Safeguard: ungradable is a disposition with a date, not a null result. It routes to mydriatic imaging or optometry and is counted in the coverage denominator, so a practice cannot improve its numbers by discarding hard images.

Over-referral floods the eye clinic

Safeguard: thresholds are set jointly with the receiving department and reconciled monthly against what the ophthalmologist actually found; the department can move the threshold, and sees the effect on its own list.

Automation bias de-skills the GP

Safeguard: the image is displayed to the clinician, not just the grade; the output is a disposition rather than a diagnosis; each practice has a named ophthalmologist of record for questions; the model's confidence is shown, and low confidence behaves like ungradable.

The images become a data asset

Safeguard: images live in the practice's clinical record under the patient's consent; no secondary use without explicit, separate, revocable consent; the oculomics read in §05 is investigational, separately consented, and never drives treatment on its own.
